Why agentic payments need an authorization layer

2026-10-16 · ~6 min · sources at the end

Agents can already pay on Stellar: x402 and MPP are live on mainnet. The open question isn't can an agent pay, it's should it be allowed to sign this specific payment. Looking at recent incidents across chains shows where an authorization layer helps, and just as clearly where it doesn't.

The Stellar anchor: Blend / YieldBlox, Feb 2026

On 2026-02-22 an attacker pushed the illiquid USTRY/USDC market on the Stellar DEX up about 100x with a tiny trade. The Reflector VWAP price followed. A YieldBlox community pool on Blend V2 accepted the inflated collateral and lent out its XLM and USDC, about $10.2M[1]. Validators froze about 48M XLM[3].

“This incident was not a Blend V2 core-contract issue. It was a pool-operator (YieldBlox DAO) configuration issue.” BlockSec[2]

Honest take: a wallet policy would not have prevented this. The attacker's own account did exactly what the attacker wanted, and the failure was pool pricing. Policy signers can only limit how much your agent puts at risk when it interacts with such a pool (allowlists, per-call caps, a daily spending_limit). Oracle configuration is a protocol problem.

Where an authorization layer does matter

AIXBT (Base, Mar 2025). An attacker got into an AI agent's operator dashboard and queued two prompts, and the agent sent 55.5 ETH (about $106k)[6]. The maintainer said it wasn't AI manipulation; the control plane was compromised. Nothing independent stood between the instruction and the money moving. That's the gap a co-signer fills: it checks the payment against the user's intent from grant time, and per-call and daily caps bound the loss either way.

ElizaOS memory injection (research, 2025). Princeton/Sentient researchers showed injected memories can trigger unauthorized transfers, and that prompt-injection defenses “only provide limited protection when stored context is corrupted”[7]. That argues for a decision made outside the agent's context, from the passkey-signed intent and the decoded transaction, with agent text treated as untrusted. It also argues for bounds that don't depend on any model.

Hot-key and signer compromise. Bybit lost about $1.5B after its signers approved a transaction that a tampered Safe{Wallet} UI disguised[4]. Phemex lost about $73M across 16 chains, likely from compromised hot-wallet keys[5]. Force Bridge lost about $3.76M after validator keys were exfiltrated. OAK's write-up points to the absence of “per-transaction velocity limits”[8]. An agent key is a hot key. With policy signers a stolen agent key isn't enough on its own, and caps bound the damage even if the AI co-signer is also compromised.

Approvals and drainers (EVM, 2025). Scam Sniffer counted $83.85M in drainer losses. Permit/Permit2 approvals accounted for 38% of losses in cases over $1M[9]. On Stellar, jev_policy can cap or allowlist SEP-41 approve, or leave it off the function allowlist entirely.

Where it doesn't: protocol bugs

KiloEx (BNB Chain, Base and others; about $7.5M per Halborn, about $8.44M per SlowMist) left a permissionless forwarder that let an attacker set oracle prices[10]. Loopscale (Solana; 5.73M USDC + 1,211 SOL, all recovered) accepted a spoofed pricing program[11]. Cetus (Sui, about $223M) had one unchecked shift[12]. These are contract and oracle bugs inside the protocol. No wallet-side policy fixes them.

The thesis is narrow on purpose. Deterministic rules bound how much; an AI co-signer adds whether a payment matches what the user asked for, and it can only ever restrict. Our proof of concept on testnet measured this on a small benchmark. It's evidence, not a guarantee.
